Network slicing lets enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC) and massive machine-type communication (mMTC) services share the same infrastructure. Most slicing controllers react to demand after it has changed. This thesis asks whether predicting traffic before bandwidth is allocated helps, and whether a decentralised multi-agent controller is a better design than a single central one.

We built a pipeline of self-supervised learning (SSL), a temporal graph neural network (TGNN) forecaster and multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), and tested it in a Digital Twin. No public dataset joins topology, per-slice traffic, quality of service and allocation actions, so we built a unified slicing environment from real Telecom Italia Milan traffic (62 days, 10{,}000 cells), with latency calibrated on a public 6G RAN telemetry dataset. Results are measured on a held-out week with controllers chosen on separate validation days.

A parameter-shared MARL controller keeps a fixed actor of 19{,}332 parameters from 9 to 100 cells, while a single central agent grows to 604{,}048 parameters and its reward falls from about 0.43 to 0.27. Against rule-based controllers whose targets were tuned on the training days, the off-policy learned controller (with a long training budget and seed averaging) is better on all seven test days at every size tested, by 0.023 to 0.026 in reward, and an on-policy (PPO-family) learner also beats a richer rule that is tuned per time of day and cell class, on every test day (by 0.009 in reward at 25 cells), whereas the off-policy learner only matches that rule. A TGNN trained on 39 days beats persistence on all 10{,}000 cells (mean squared error lower by 18\%, 33\% and 31\% for eMBB, URLLC and mMTC) and raises the reward of the rule-based controller by about 0.03, recovering about one fifth of the gap to perfect knowledge of the next interval; it has not been shown to help the learned controller. Self-supervised pretraining, evaluated on de-duplicated data across label budgets, gave no gain over strong raw-feature baselines, and we report it as a negative result. The Digital Twin works as an approval gate that protects the reward from poor proposals, and a packet-level Simu5G sweep agrees with the calibrated QoS model on ordering but not on the shape of the delay curve. We also provide a 393.6-million-row Digital Twin dataset with six what-if scenarios and an interactive monitor, Slice Twin, that is published on the web.
