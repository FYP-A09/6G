\section{Allocation Problem}
\label{sec:allocation_problem}
The last learned component distributes the capacity of each cell between the eMBB, URLLC and mMTC slices. Every cell has an agent. It observes 17 local quantities (demand, utilisation and QoS of each slice, the previous allocation, time of day, and the forecast when one is provided) and outputs four non-negative numbers, normalised by a softmax into the shares of eMBB, URLLC, mMTC and idle capacity. The environment of Chapter~\ref{chap.4} then computes the QoS, the SLA outcomes and the reward. Cells interact only through interference, because the usable capacity of a cell falls when its neighbours are loaded.

\section{Scalable MARL Design}
\label{sec:scalable_marl}
All cells use one parameter-shared actor, a network with 19{,}332 parameters that maps a cell's own observation to its allocation. Since the actor never sees other cells' observations, it can be copied to any network size. Training uses a centralised neighbourhood critic: it evaluates a cell's observation and action together with the mean observation and mean action of its grid neighbours. Each agent's reward mixes in its neighbours' rewards, which encourages local coordination.

The learning algorithm is a MADDPG-style actor--critic \cite{lowe2017} with a replay buffer of 50{,}000 transitions, a discount factor of 0.9, soft target updates with $\tau=0.01$, learning rates of $3\times10^{-4}$ for the actor and $10^{-3}$ for the critic, and exploration noise added in logit space. The earlier B5G trainer used the observed reward as a one-step target, because its offline samples are contextual-bandit decisions rather than interactions with a simulator.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:marl_protocol}
Every result in this chapter follows one protocol. Agents train on days 1 to 40 of the recorded data. Days 41 to 44 are used only to choose a checkpoint or tune a rule. The test is the held-out week 16 to 22 December 2013, one reward per day, and the table entries are the mean over those seven days. Differences between two controllers are paired by day, and the interval quoted is a 95\% interval on the mean difference with a $t$ value for six degrees of freedom. Consecutive days are not independent, so these intervals describe the variation between days of this one week; they do not cover a different week or a different city. Learned policies are also averaged over several seeds, and the number of seeds is stated in each table.

\section{Comparison with Properly Tuned Rules}
\label{sec:marl_vs_tuned}
Learning must also be compared with the strongest simple controller, not only with equal split. The expert rule gives URLLC and mMTC enough capacity to keep their utilisation at a target, and the rest to eMBB. Its default targets were chosen by hand. Two tuned versions were therefore built, using only the training days.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simple tuned rule.} Two utilisation targets (URLLC and mMTC) are chosen by grid search on the training days. At 25 cells the search picks 0.4 and 0.4 and raises the held-out reward of the forecast-fed rule from 0.804 to 0.878.
\item \textbf{Rich tuned rule.} The targets are chosen separately for each four-hour block of the day and each of three cell classes (by traffic level), 18 pairs in total.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:marl_scaling} shows that at 1{,}000 episodes the shared MARL controller is clearly better than the untuned rules but loses to the simple tuned rule at 25 and 100 cells. The budget was then raised to a long protocol: 4{,}000 episodes, the checkpoint with the best validation reward, and an average of several seeds' actors in logit space. \section{Training Variants}
\label{sec:marl_variants}
Several ways of improving the learner were tried at 25 cells (Table~\ref{tab:marl_variants}). Adding explicit neighbour communication to the actor, an expert-residual policy (the actor outputs a correction to the rule's allocation), an expert-regularised loss in the style of TD3+BC \cite{fujimoto2021minimalist} and richer observations were all tried. None of them is clearly better than the plain shared actor trained for the long protocol and then averaged (the on-policy learners of Section~\ref{sec:marl_ppo} are the exception); an actor given richer observations does not beat the rich tuned rule (a difference of $-0.007\pm0.009$, better on 2 of 7 days). \section{On-Policy Baselines}
\label{sec:marl_ppo}
Independent PPO (IPPO) and a MAPPO-style variant \cite{schulman2017ppo, yu2022mappo, dewitt2020ippo} were implemented for comparison with the off-policy design. The policy is a Gaussian over the four logits, advantages use generalised advantage estimation \cite{schulman2016gae} with $\lambda=0.95$ and $\gamma=0.9$ on the team reward, the clipping parameter is 0.2, and each update uses 10 episodes. IPPO gives each cell's actor and value function only its own observation. In MAPPO the value function also sees the mean observation of the cell's grid neighbours, the neighbourhood form of a centralised critic, as in the DDPG critic, while the actor stays local. Both were trained for 8{,}000 episodes with the same checkpoint rule. Table~\ref{tab:marl_ppo} gives the outcome. All 12 runs end at almost the same reward, between 0.907 and 0.908, whichever variant or seed is used. This is higher than the off-policy controller of the previous sections (0.904) and higher than the rich tuned rule (0.899): averaged over the runs the difference to the rich rule is +0.009 $\pm$ 0.002, with a gain on 7 of 7 days, and every single run beats the rich rule on all seven days. The true-next-demand rule, which uses information no deployable controller has, is still higher at 0.920. IPPO and MAPPO give the same result, so in this environment the neighbourhood term in the value function brings no measurable benefit. The near-identical outcome of independent seeds suggests that the on-policy learner reliably finds the same good allocation behaviour, and that the remaining gap to the oracle comes from demand that cannot be predicted ten minutes ahead.

\section{Earlier B5G MADDPG Development}
\label{sec:b5g-maddpg}
Before the unified Milan environment existed, MADDPG was implemented on the B5G network-slicing release. The environment loads graph, routing and slice data and creates one observation for each node that originates at least one flow. The policy uses a shared actor and a critic that evaluates the local state and allocation. Initial comparisons included equal allocation, a rule-based heuristic and an untrained actor.

During development, slice identifiers were found to restart across slice types. Using the identifier alone as the agent key merged the records of eMBB, URLLC and mMTC and invalidated the statistics, so results affected by the error were excluded. The B5G release also lacks populated latency and packet-loss fields, which an SLA-based reward needs. These experiments therefore show that the replay, training and evaluation pipeline runs, but they do not show that the learned policy improves real QoS.

\section{Chapter Conclusion}
\label{sec:marl-conclusion}
Three statements are supported. First, a parameter-shared actor with a neighbourhood critic keeps its size and its reward when the network grows from 9 to 100 cells, whereas a central agent does not. Second, with a fair training budget, validation-based checkpointing and seed averaging, the learned controller beats a simply tuned expert rule on every test day at 9, 25 and 100 cells. Third, the off-policy controller only matches a richer, time-aware tuned rule, whereas the PPO-family learners, trained for 8{,}000 episodes, beat that rule on every test day in every run at 25 cells. Finally, learned controllers need retraining or scenario mixing when traffic shifts strongly. The question of whether a forecast helps the learned controller is taken up in Chapter~\ref{chap.8}.
