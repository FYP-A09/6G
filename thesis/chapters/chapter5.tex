\section{Motivation and Objective}
Self-supervised learning (SSL) lets a model learn useful descriptions of network telemetry without a label for every observation. We hoped these descriptions could feed the later stages, a classifier or the forecaster, and that they would matter most when only a few labels are available. This chapter checks that idea. It describes the encoder, the data problems that first made our results look worse than they were, and a study that asks the question SSL is meant to answer: does pretraining help when only a few flows are labelled?

\section{Pretext Tasks}
\subsection{First implementation: masked reconstruction}
Our first encoder was trained on 5G-NIDD. Eleven context features (protocol, TCP state, TTL and hop attributes, among others) were treated as categories with an 8-dimensional embedding each. Fourteen volume and rate features (packet and byte counts, mean packet sizes, load and rate) were hidden with probability 0.30 and had to be rebuilt from the visible ones. The encoder had two linear layers of 128 and 64 units and gave a 64-dimensional embedding, and a decoder with one hidden layer of 128 units rebuilt the hidden values. The loss is the mean squared error over the hidden positions only,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(\hat{x}_j-x_j)^2 ,
\label{eq:mask_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of masked positions, $x_j$ the original value and $\hat{x}_j$ the reconstruction. Training used Adam with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, a batch size of 512 and 10 epochs, on all 1.2 million flows.

\subsection{Re-evaluation: two pretext tasks on cleaned data}
For the final study we redid the pretraining with two standard tabular pretext tasks. Both use the same encoder (83 inputs, two hidden layers of 256 units, a 64-dimensional output), so the comparison between them is fair.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Masked-feature modelling.} About 30\% of the entries of each flow are swapped with the values of the same feature from other flows. The encoder has to recover the original values and also say which entries were swapped \cite{yoon2020vime}.
\item \textbf{Contrastive learning (SCARF).} Each flow is paired with a copy in which 60\% of the features are replaced by values taken from other random flows. A contrastive loss over the batch (temperature 0.5, batch size 512) pulls the two views of the same flow together \cite{bahri2022scarf}.
\end{itemize}
Both were trained without any label on the 80\% pool of flows kept for training (326{,}924 flows).

\section{Cleaning the Data}
The first evaluation reported a macro-F1 of 0.708. Two data problems explain why that number was so low, and fixing them changed the baseline far more than any SSL variant.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Duplicate flows.} Of the 1{,}215{,}890 flows in 5G-NIDD, 807{,}235 (66\%) are exact copies of other flows. With a random split, identical flows land in both the training and the test set. Once they are removed, 408{,}655 unique flows remain, in nine classes (benign and eight attack types).
\item \textbf{Identifier columns.} The row index, the sequence number and the offset tell where a flow sits in the capture, and that gives away its label, so we dropped them. The other numeric features are heavy-tailed counts, so we took their logarithm, and we one-hot encoded the categorical ones. That leaves 83 inputs.
\end{itemize}
A stratified 80/20 split leaves 326{,}924 flows for pretraining and 81{,}731 for testing. We had also found earlier that the source file is stored in blocks of identical labels. A split by position therefore put 99.88\% of one class into the test set and gave an accuracy of about 1\%. The stratified split removes that problem as well.

\section{Label-Efficiency Study}
Pretraining should help when labels are scarce, so we trained classifiers on 50, 200, 1{,}000, 5{,}000 and 20{,}000 labelled flows, and on all labels. Each budget up to 5{,}000 was repeated with five random draws of the labelled flows, and larger budgets with two. The classifiers were logistic regression, a small multilayer perceptron and LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} on the raw features; logistic regression on the frozen embedding; the pretrained encoder fine-tuned with a classification head; and logistic regression on the raw features together with the embedding. The metric is macro-averaged F1 over the nine classes.

Three observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:ssl_label_efficiency} and Figure~\ref{fig:ssl_label_efficiency}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The raw features are already strong. With all labels, logistic regression reaches 0.926 and LightGBM 0.936, compared with 0.708 in our first evaluation. Most of the earlier gap therefore came from duplicates and leaked identifiers, not from the classifier.
\item Pretraining does not beat the best raw-feature model at any budget. With 50 and 200 labels a raw-feature perceptron or logistic regression is ahead of the fine-tuned encoder (for example 0.694 and 0.704 against 0.688 at 50 labels), and a linear classifier on the frozen embedding alone is clearly worse. With all labels the fine-tuned contrastive encoder ties the same network trained from scratch (0.929 against 0.925) and stays below LightGBM.
\item Adding the embedding to the raw features helps a linear model only a little, by at most 0.014 at 1{,}000 to 5{,}000 labels, and not at all with 20{,}000 or more.
\end{enumerate}
LightGBM is the weakest method at 50 labels, because boosted trees need more data. That is why we also compare against the neural and linear raw-feature models.

\section{SSL on the Milan Forecast}
\label{sec:milan_ssl}
In our first forecasting experiment (Chapter~\ref{chap.6}) a TGNN fed with 64-dimensional embeddings of Milan traffic scored the same validation error as one fed with the raw features (0.000616 against 0.000612). That was one day and one seed, so we repeated the test on the city forecaster. The embedding is now 16-dimensional and describes the input window of one cell: the last six intervals of demand, the same time on the previous day, the time of day and a weekend flag. The encoder is pretrained without labels. It hides 30\% of the entries of a window and rebuilds them, using windows from the training days only. It is then frozen, and its output goes to the output layer of the forecaster. Table~\ref{tab:ssl_milan} gives the outcome. Both variants use the same data, split, scaling and number of training steps; the only difference is the embedding. The pretrained embedding does not help: with it the eMBB improvement is about one percentage point lower (15\% against 16\%), and URLLC and mMTC are within half a point of the raw forecaster. Each variant was trained twice with different seeds, and the run-to-run difference is of the same size as the gap, so we conclude that the embedding adds nothing measurable.

\section{Interpretation}
\label{sec:ssl_interpretation}
The finding is the same on both tasks: the raw features already hold what the downstream models need, and masked or contrastive pretraining adds very little. A likely reason is that these are low-dimensional, hand-made features and not raw packets or long sequences. Pretraining is known to pay off on high-dimensional raw inputs, where no one has engineered features. Other work on tabular data reports the same thing, namely that the advantage of pretraining often disappears against well-tuned baselines \cite{rubachev2022revisiting}. We therefore report SSL as a negative result of this project. What this chapter contributes is the cleaned evaluation: de-duplicated data, no leaked columns, a design that varies the number of labels, and strong raw-feature baselines. The SSL stage stays in the pipeline of Chapter~\ref{chap.4} because the literature recommends it, but we do not claim that it improves the forecasts or the allocation.
